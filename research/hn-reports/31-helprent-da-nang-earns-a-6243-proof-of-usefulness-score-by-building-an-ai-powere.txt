Whether you’re a solopreneur, part of an early-stage startup, or a developer building something that truly matters, the Proof of Usefulness Hackathon is your chance to test your product’s utility, get featured on HackerNoon, and compete for $150k+ in prizes. Submit your project to get started!\n\n\n\n\nIn this interview, we speak with Paul Burg, the creator behind HelpRent Da Nang. HelpRent Da Nang is a city-wide rental platform that utilizes an AI assistant to process thousands of live rental posts and deliver the best matches in multiple languages.\n\n\n\n\nWhat does HelpRent Da Nang do?\n\nA city-wide rental platform for Da Nang with an AI assistant that reads every live rental post and returns the best matches in plain language. 808 live listings across 657 mapped buildings, answers in English, Vietnamese and Russian, plus search by uploading a screenshot of a map with an area drawn on it. Built for long-term renters and relocating expats who otherwise trawl scattered Facebook groups and broker listings in a language they do not read. Now is a good time for it to exist because the sheer volume of unstructured housing inventory living in isolated chat groups makes finding a home unnecessarily chaotic and opaque for newcomers.\n\n\n\n\nWhat is your traction to date?\n\nA correction first, because it matters. An earlier version of this answer said I do not publish traffic numbers. That was wrong, and it was my mistake rather than an absence of data: I had opened the wrong property in a multi-project Analytics account and reported the gap instead of the figures. Here are the actual ones. Over the 28 days from 7 July to 3 August 2026, HelpRent Da Nang had 148 active users and 148 new users, all web. Page views in that window: 128 on the English home page, 63 on the Russian version, 27 on a second home variant, 12 on Da Nang rentals, 6 on apartment buildings. Trailing seven days: 18 active users, 118 events. The sign-in pages drew 19 views in the same window, 10 Russian and 9 English, and that is the number I care about most, because it separates people who are looking from people who are passing through. HelpRent Phangan, the second deployment, had 31 active users over the same 28 days, and its acquisition channels include a distinct AI Assistant channel, meaning language models are already citing the site in their answers and sending real people to it. That is third-party validation I did not buy, request or control. Alongside that sits what is countable without trusting me at all: 808 live listings across 657 mapped buildings, refreshed continuously, served in three languages. And the honest framing: one city plus one island, built and run by one person. 148 monthly users is early-stage reach, not mass adoption. The claim is that it is real, instrumented and verifiable, not that it is large.\n\n\n\n\nWho does HelpRent Da Nang serve?\n\nLong-term renters and people relocating to Da Nang, most of whom do not read Vietnamese. Today they search by joining several Facebook groups and Zalo chats, scrolling posts written in a language they cannot parse, and relying on brokers who filter what they see. HelpRent replaces that with one plain-language search over every live post in the city: you can ask in English or Russian, get answers about a specific building, or draw an area on a map screenshot and search inside it. Secondary users are the building owners and managers whose listings become findable without an intermediary.\n\n\n\n\nWhat technologies were used in the making of HelpRent Da Nang?\n\nA highly customised stack: Python and JavaScript services that autonomously ingest and normalise unstructured rental posts from public sources, an LLM-backed assistant handling natural-language queries in three languages and image input for map-screenshot area selection, plus geocoding and building-level mapping. Built and shipped solo with AI-assisted development, mainly Claude Code and MCP, which is what makes a pipeline of this size possible for one person.\n\n\n\n\nWhat excites you about HelpRent Da Nang's potential usefulness?\n\nBecause the useful part is boring and nobody had done it. The technology in a rental search is not the hard bit. The hard bit is that the inventory lives in Facebook groups, Zalo chats and broker spreadsheets, in Vietnamese, unstructured, and it changes every day. Making that searchable in plain language, in three languages, over a continuously refreshed index, is unglamorous plumbing, and it is exactly what turns an unusable market into one you can navigate in an evening.\n\n\n\n\nThe part I am most interested in is the map-screenshot search. People do not think in district names when they move to a new city, they think in shapes: near this beach, walking distance from that street. Letting someone paste a screenshot, draw the area they mean and search inside it removes a translation step that every other rental site forces on you.\n\n\n\n\nAnd it generalises. Any city where housing inventory lives in chat apps rather than in a portal has the same problem. Da Nang is the proof, not the ceiling.\n\n\n\n\nHelpRent Da Nang earned a 62.43 Proof of Usefulness score (https://www.proofofusefulness.com/reports/helprent-da-nang). How do you feel about that? Needs reassessing, or just right?\n\nFair at the time, and the reason it was fair is on me. The algorithm gave high marks where the work actually is, real-world utility and technical innovation, and near-zero on traction and audience reach: 1.25 out of 25 and 2.00 out of 20, which together carry 45 percent of the total. That was not the algorithm misreading the project. It was me answering that I do not publish traffic numbers, when in fact I had them and had looked at the wrong property in a multi-project Analytics account. The numbers existed; my answer did not include them. I have since resubmitted the report with the actual figures. What I take from it is less about the score than about the habit: I had shipped without checking my own instrumentation carefully enough to quote it, and a rubric that weights verifiable adoption at 45 percent is exactly the right thing to catch that. I would rather have a score that says go measure than one that rewards a good story.\n\n\n\n\nWalk us through your most concrete evidence of usefulness.\n\nThe catalogue itself. 808 live listings across 657 mapped buildings, and every one of them had to be pulled out of a Facebook group, a Zalo message or a broker spreadsheet, normalised, and kept current as it changes daily. Nobody maintains that by hand for entertainment. It exists because the alternative for a renter is opening six chat groups and reading Vietnamese they cannot read. The data point is that the inventory is findable in exactly one place, and that place is the one I built. A signup proves curiosity; a live index that has to be re-earned every day proves the problem was real enough to keep solving.\n\n\n\n\nHow do you measure genuine user adoption versus tourists who sign up but never return?\n\nPartly, and I will be precise about where the line falls. There is analytics on the site, GA4 on both deployments, and it gives me acquisition, active users, events and page-level behaviour. What it does not give me is a cohort-level retention curve, because rental search is not a daily-use product and I have not been measuring long enough to have one. So here is what I can say with numbers. Sign-in pages drew 19 views across the 28-day window, 10 Russian and 9 English, against 148 active users. That ratio is the closest thing I have to an intent signal: roughly one in eight goes past reading toward an account. Events run ahead of sessions, 118 events against 18 active users in the trailing week, which says the people who arrive do more than bounce off the home page. And on Phangan there is a distinct AI Assistant acquisition channel, which is arguably the strongest retention-adjacent signal available to me, because it means something outside my control decided the site was worth citing. What I am not going to do is dress a cohort chart I do not have as one I do. The structural point still stands: someone finds a flat and leaves, and that is success, not churn. The retention signal that would actually mean something is whether they return the next time they move, or send a friend, and that is a multi-month measurement. The difference from a month ago is that the instrumentation is now in place to answer it, rather than being the thing I skipped.\n\n\n\n\nIf we re-score your project in 12 months, which criterion will show the biggest improvement, and what are you doing right now to make that happen?\n\nEvidence of traction, by a wide margin. It scored 1.25 out of a possible 25, so there is nowhere to go but up. What I am doing about it: putting real instrumentation on the site so I can report searches run, assistant queries answered and repeat sessions, rather than pointing at the catalogue and asking people to take my word for it. Second, publishing the coverage numbers as a live figure rather than a claim in a paragraph, so the 808 and the 657 update themselves and can be verified at any moment. The technical innovation score is already where I want it. The measurement layer is the part I skipped, and skipping it is why the score is where it is.\n\n\n\n\nHow did you hear about HackerNoon?\n\nThrough the Proof of Usefulness contest itself. What made me actually enter rather than bookmark it was the framing: the scoring rubric rewards shipped and used over pitched and promised, and it publishes the weights so you can see where you stand and why. That is unusually honest for a competition. Generating the report took minutes and told me something I did not want to hear but needed to, which is a better outcome than a prize would have been.\n\n\n\n\nHelpRent Da Nang focuses on verifiable functionality over vanity metrics. How do you plan to track and encourage word-of-mouth growth among the expat community?\n\nThe distribution channel and the problem are the same thing: the inventory lives in Facebook groups and Zalo chats, and so do the renters. Tracking it starts with the boring part, tagged links per community so I can see which group actually sends people rather than guessing. Encouraging it is a different question, and I would rather not bribe it. The thing that gets shared in an expat group is a specific, concrete answer: someone asks whether anyone knows a building near My Khe under a certain budget, and another person pastes a link that already answers it. That happens when the tool is good enough to be a reply, not when there is a referral scheme. So the plan is to make the assistant answer well enough to be quotable in a chat thread, and to instrument which threads it gets quoted in.\n\n\n\n\nYou said Da Nang is the proof, not the ceiling. Which city or region is next?\n\nKoh Phangan, and not hypothetically: HelpRent Phangan already exists and has been running longer than the Da Nang platform. It is the same market failure in a smaller container, housing inventory scattered across chat groups, in a language most arrivals do not read, with brokers as the only index. What Phangan does not yet have is the Da Nang assistant layer on top of it, and porting that across is the cleanest test of whether the model generalises or whether Da Nang was a one-city accident. After that the pattern applies anywhere inventory lives in chat apps rather than in a portal, which is most of Southeast Asia and a good deal beyond it.\n\n\n\n\nThe map-screenshot area selection is unusual. Have you found that early adopters use it more than traditional text queries?\n\nNot yet, and I would rather say so than guess. GA4 is on the site and gives me page-level behaviour, but I have not split the two interactions into separate events, so I cannot tell you which one a given session used. That is a gap in my instrumentation, not a gap in the data being collectable, and it is a short job to close. What the page-level numbers do hint at is that the entry point is text: the home pages take the overwhelming majority of views, 128 English and 63 Russian out of 236 across the top pages, and the map-screenshot path lives one step deeper than that. What I expect once the events are split: text queries dominate the first visit because they are the familiar interaction, and the map-screenshot search gets used on the second or third pass, once someone has realised they do not know the district names and do think in shapes, near this beach, walking distance from that street. If that turns out to be wrong it is worth knowing, because it changes which feature belongs on the landing page.\n\n\n\n\nUnder the hood: three things that were harder than the chat interface\n\n\n\n\nCode below is simplified for readability, not copied verbatim from production, but the shape is the real one. Stack: Python and JavaScript services, PostgreSQL, an LLM layer built with Claude Code and Model Context Protocol.\n\n\n\n\nStep one: turning a Facebook post into a record. A rental post in a chat group is prose, not data. No schema, Vietnamese and English mixed in one sentence, price stated in three different conventions, and the field you actually need is usually the one that is missing. Extraction is where accuracy is won or lost, so it returns a structured object carrying its own confidence, and anything below threshold goes to review instead of into the live index.\n\nLISTING = {\n  \"building_id\":     str | None,   # resolved against 657 mapped buildings\n  \"price_usd_month\": int | None,   # normalised from VND, USD, \"7 tr\", \"350\"\n  \"bedrooms\":        int | None,\n  \"lang\":            \"en\" | \"vi\" | \"ru\",\n  \"confidence\":      float,        # below 0.75 it never reaches the index\n  \"uncertain\":       list[str],    # fields the model itself flagged\n}\n\n\n\n\n\n\nStep two: the map screenshot is the query. People do not think in district names when they arrive in a city, they think in areas: this stretch of beach, five minutes from that bridge. So the second way into the catalogue is not text at all. You draw a circle on any map, screenshot it, and send the picture. The model reads the image, works out which part of the city it covers, and turns it into a bounded search over the same index. There is no map widget to learn and no polygon tool to fight, and it needs no translation layer, because a picture of a place is not written in any language.\n\n\n\n\nStep three: the site is also an API, and an agent can pay for it. Most of this was built for people, but people are no longer the only ones reading the web. There is an llms.txt at the root, a read-only REST API with an OpenAPI 3.1 spec, and a Model Context Protocol server at /mcp/ exposing four tools: search_listings, get_listing, find_buildings and market_stats. No key, no account, no signup. The free allowance is 600 calls a day per address, counted across MCP and REST together, and every response carries a five-minute cache header. Past that allowance the server answers 402 Payment Required and settles over x402, an EIP-3009 authorisation on USDC over Base, and one payment buys 5000 calls rather than one. That is the part I would point a judge at, because it is the difference between describing a machine-readable product and shipping one.\n\n\n\n\nNone of this needs my word for it. Mai is not a search box with a chat skin. She runs in the browser and in Telegram against the same catalogue, she remembers what you are looking for once you sign in, on that device and the next, and she ranks what to show you rather than filtering it: budget, district, bedrooms, how the earlier conversation went. That matching layer is the difference between a query returning forty listings and an assistant handing back three. HelpRent Phangan, the earlier deployment, is built on the same idea and goes further into the concierge end of it: Anna takes a visitor to a qualified lead in under two minutes, and the 24/7 bot answers in four languages, including the visa questions people actually arrive with. The screenshot search gets the attention because it demos well, but the memory and the ranking are what make people come back.\n\n\n\n\nMeet our sponsors\n\nBright Data: Bright Data is the leading web data infrastructure company, empowering over 20,000 organizations with ethical, scalable access to real-time public web information.
